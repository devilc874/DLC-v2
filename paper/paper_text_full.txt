DLC: A Multi-Model, Adaptive-Precision Near-Lossless 
Compression Engine for High-Frequency Float64 
Time-Series Telemetry 
Abstract - Most modern industrial sensors, IoT gateways, phasor 
units and scientific instruments emit their readings as IEEE 754 
double-precision (float64) values. When those bytes are fed into a 
general-purpose dictionary compressor like GZIP or ZLIB, the 
resulting compression ratio is typically close to one. The cause is 
structural: roughly the bottom 30–40 bits of every mantissa carry 
hardware thermal noise, so the byte stream contains very few 
repeating substrings for a Lempel–Ziv dictionary to exploit. We call 
this the Entropy Trap. Strictly lossless float coders such as Gorilla 
and Chimp move past dictionary matching with XOR-based delta 
encoding, but they still have to faithfully encode the noise floor and 
so their ratios on real sensor traces remain modest. This paper 
describes DLC (Delta-Linear Compression), a near-lossless engine 
that works in the mathematical domain rather than the byte domain. 
Each block is broken into variance-homogeneous windows by a 
greedy doubling search; for every window the engine fits six 
predictive models in parallel Linear, Quadratic, XOR-Delta, 
Constant, Sinusoidal and Predictive-XOR and picks the one with the 
smallest Sum-of-Squared-Residuals, breaking ties in favour of 
simpler or lossless variants. The residuals are then quantized at a 
per-window precision (8–20 bits) and run through six entropy-coding 
strategies in a tournament: Zigzag-Varint, Fixed-Width Bitpack, 
Delta-of-Residuals, MAD Outlier Separation, Run-Length and Delta-
of-Delta. A whole-block DoD fast path gives the engine an extra 
escape hatch when windowing would only add overhead. Two 
implementations share the same .dlc binary specification: a NumPy-
based Python reference prototype and a C++17 production engine 
that uses std::async to bypass the GIL. Across 21 synthetic and real-
world float64 datasets (the Numenta Anomaly Benchmark, Jena 
Climate atmospheric records, and several large industrial and 
round-trip recovery traces), at a strict 16-bit precision that keeps the 
maximum per-sample reconstruction error below 8×10⁻⁶, DLC 
compresses smaller than every baseline Gorilla, DoD (16-bit), RLE, 
GZIP and ZLIB on 19 of 21 datasets (90.5%), reaching 2355.7× on a 
1-million-sample linear ramp and 102.0× on a 1-million-sample 
recovered signal. The two losses are a network-packet counter trace 
where ZLIB's dictionary dominates (8.5× vs. DLC's 5.7×) and a low-
magnitude taxi-demand signal where Gorilla's XOR encoding edges 
out DLC (3.6× vs. 3.4×). For signals that select only closed-form 
models (Constant, Linear, Quadratic) the two engines emit byte-
identical .dlc files; for the remaining cases their outputs cross-decode 
inside the bound. 
Keywords: time-series compression, floating-point compression, 
IoT telemetry, predictive coding, residual quantization, adaptive 
precision, variance-guided segmentation, parallel pipeline, IEEE 
754, near-lossless 
I. INTRODUCTION 
Telemetry volumes have grown faster than storage budgets 
for several years now. By IDC's reckoning, connected sensors 
and industrial IoT will be producing well above 180 zettabytes 
a year by the end of this decade, and a large share of that data 
lands on disk as 8-byte IEEE 754 doubles [1]. The reason is 
straightforward: any scientific or engineering pipeline that 
touches phasor measurements, vibration spectra, vehicle 
diagnostics, or process-control loops would rather not throw 
away bits of mantissa to save bytes. The bandwidth bill, 
however, lands somewhere usually at the edge gateway, where 
compression has to happen in tight latency and power 
windows. The unfortunate twist is that the standard tools for 
the job, namely GZIP and ZLIB, contribute almost nothing on 
this kind of data. 
The reason is rooted in the layout of a float64 value, which 
works against dictionary methods. One sign bit, eleven 
exponent bits, and a fifty-two-bit mantissa [2]; for a 
continuous physical signal, temperature, current, pressure, 
displacement, the usual list successive samples agree on most 
of the high-order mantissa bits, since the underlying value 
moves only a little between ticks. The bottom 30 to 40 bits are 
a different story. Those are occupied by hardware thermal 
noise plus the quantization residue of the ADC, and they look 
statistically random from one sample to the next. A Lempel–
Ziv dictionary [3] scanning the resulting byte stream therefore 
finds essentially no repeating substrings to exploit, no matter 
how aggressively it is tuned. We have taken to calling this the 
Entropy Trap, because the very thing that makes a sensor 
reading scientifically meaningful its noise floor is exactly 
what defeats a general-purpose compressor on it. 
The literature has split, broadly, into two camps. The first 
works in the bit domain and refuses to lose any information. 
Gorilla [4] and Chimp [5] are the canonical examples: each 
new float64 value is XOR-ed against its predecessor and the 
resulting word, which usually has many leading zeros, is 
packed with a variable-length header that records exactly how 
many leading and trailing zero bits to skip. The schemes work 
they are deployed in production time-series databases but the 
random low-order bits still travel through the encoder 
untouched, and on noisy real-world data the achievable ratio 
plateaus quickly. Liakos et al. give this phenomenon a name, 

=== PAGE 2 ===
the “Entropy Wall” [5]. The second camp accepts a bounded 
loss in exchange for stronger compression: SZ [6], [7] and 
ZFP [8] both predict each sample, take the residual, and 
quantize that residual to a user-supplied tolerance. SZ3 is 
impressively accurate on HPC simulation grids; on a battery-
powered edge gateway, however, its modular pipeline starts to 
creak [17]. Sprintz [9], which targets time series, is fast but is 
optimised for 8- and 16-bit integer sensors and falls back to a 
poor predictor on 64-bit floats. Newer semantic-extraction 
work like Shrink [10] does well on the macro-shape of the 
signal but does not have a tight story for the random residual 
that remains after the macro-shape is subtracted off. 
Before turning to what we built, it is worth stating plainly 
what we set out to do. The work has five research objectives, 
and they have been the guard rails for every design call we 
made. 
RO1 Bypass the Entropy Trap. Move compression off the 
byte stream entirely and into a model-fit / residual-encode 
pipeline, so that the noisy lower mantissa stops 
sabotaging the dictionary. 
RO2 Hold a strict per-sample error bound. Guarantee that 
every reconstructed sample lies within ±8×10⁻⁶ of the 
original, on every supported telemetry type, with no quiet 
exceptions for outliers or boundary windows. 
RO3 Adapt at run time, not at compile time. The 
compressor should pick its window size, its predictor, its 
quantization width, and its entropy strategy per window, 
instead of being tuned once for an assumed signal class. 
RO4 Specify correctness in the format, not the language. 
Maintain a Python reference engine and a C++17 
production engine that agree on the .dlc binary down to 
the byte where they can, and inside the 8×10⁻⁶ envelope

DLC (Delta-Linear Compression) is our attempt to meet 
those five objectives inside a single engine. The main 
contributions of the design are the following. 
1) Six predictive models, picked per window by SSR. The 
window-level encoder fits all six candidates Linear, 
Quadratic, XOR-Delta, Constant, Sinusoidal, Predictive-
XOR and keeps whichever produces the smallest sum-of-
squared-residuals. When two models tie within a small 
margin, a hand-tuned preference rule sends ties to the 
cheaper or to the lossless option. Earlier schemes (Gorilla, 
Chimp, Sprintz) commit to a single predictor up front; we 
found that the right predictor varies window by window, 
often inside the same recording. 
2) A windowing pass that follows the variance. Window 
boundaries are chosen by a greedy doubling search with 
binary-search refinement and an explicit stagnation guard. 
Quiet, slowly-changing stretches end up inside long 
windows; transients and steps land in short ones. This is 
what lets the multi-model selector make a real choice 
without it the model fits would be dominated by signal 
heterogeneity inside the window. 
3) Adaptive precision, 8 to 20 bits, stored per window. Each 
window writes its own quantization precision into the 
binary stream. Smooth segments pay 8 bits per residual; 
the loud ones pay 16. The global per-sample error bound 
is unaffected it is enforced by the lower bound (p_floor = 
16 for the strict 8×10⁻⁶ contract) regardless of what the 
window-local optimum picks. 
4) Six entropy strategies, trial-encoded, smallest wins. 
Once the residuals are quantized, all six entropy coders 
run on them: Zigzag-Varint, Fixed-Width Bitpack, Delta-
of-Residuals, MAD Outlier Separation, Run-Length, 
Delta-of-Delta. We keep the byte string that comes out 
shortest. Including DoD here means the engine is a 
functional superset of the timestamp encoder used by 
InfluxDB and Prometheus. 
5) A whole-block DoD fast path. Before any of the 
windowing logic runs, the engine tries one shortcut: 
encode the entire block as a single Constant(mean) 
window with DoD. If that comes out shorter than the 
windowed result, we use it and discard the per-window 
metadata. The check is cheap and pays for itself on long, 
gentle ramps where windowing has nothing to do. 
6) A binary contract that holds across two languages. 
There is a Python reference (NumPy-backed) and a 
C++17 production engine. Both write the same 64-byte 
header and 8-byte CRC-32 footer; for windows that pick a 
closed-form model the two engines emit byte-identical 
files; for the rest they cross-decode inside the 8×10⁻⁶ 
bound. We treat this as a hard CI requirement, not an 
aspiration. 
7) Real multi-core scaling in the C++ engine. The Python 
prototype is GIL-bound and plateaus at a few tens of 
MB/s. The C++ engine uses std::async with an explicit 
future-collection loop that preserves block-index order, so 
its 
output

synthetic edge cases (sine, ramp, noisy ramp, Lorenz attractor,

II. RELATED WORK AND LITERATURE SURVEY 
The literature relevant to DLC sits in five overlapping 
pockets. There is the strictly-lossless XOR/delta line of float 
compressors that databases like InfluxDB and Prometheus 
made famous; there is the error-bounded lossy line that grew 
out of HPC simulation needs; there is the time-series-specific 
work targeted at integer or narrow-bit sensor data; there is 
recent edge/IIoT work that adds change-point detection or 
sampling on top of predictive coding; and there are the cross-
domain benchmarks and the foundational standards on which 
all of this rests. We touch each in turn before identifying 
where DLC fits. 
A. Lossless XOR/Delta-Based Float Compression 
Lindstrom and Isenburg's FPC [12] introduced the modern 
paradigm of context-based prediction followed by XOR 
encoding for float64, achieving high throughput by 
maintaining a finite-context predictor and storing leading-zero 
counts of XOR residuals. Burtscher and Ratanaworabhan 
extended FPC to higher throughput on scientific data [13]. 
Pelkonen et al.'s Gorilla [4] adopted a simpler delta-of-delta 
encoding for timestamps and a XOR encoding for values, 
becoming the de facto standard for high-cardinality time-series 
databases. Liakos et al.'s Chimp [5] tightened Gorilla's bit 
packing and trailing-zero handling, but explicitly identified the 
limitation that strict losslessness preserves the random 
hardware noise of the lower mantissa  what they termed the 
Entropy Wall. None of these methods adapt the prediction 
strategy to local signal geometry: they apply XOR uniformly 
regardless of whether the underlying signal is a smooth ramp 
or chaotic noise. 
B. Error-Bounded Lossy Scientific Compression 
Di and Cappello introduced SZ [6], a prediction-based 
error-bounded lossy compressor for HPC datasets, later 
refined as SZ3 [7] into a modular framework for composing 
prediction and quantization stages. ZFP by Lindstrom [8] 
partitions arrays into 4³ blocks and applies a block transform 
with bit-plane truncation, providing fixed-rate or fixed-
accuracy modes. SZ3 and ZFP are state-of-the-art for 
scientific simulation data but are computationally heavy: 
SZ3's modular composition incurs per-stage overhead that 
limits throughput to the low MB/s on edge hardware [9], and 
ZFP's block transform is tuned for grid-structured spatial data 
rather than streaming time series. 
C. Integer and Sensor Time-Series Compression 
Blalock, Madden, and Guttag's Sprintz [9] is a high-
throughput predictive coder for IoT sensor data, but it is 
optimized for 8- and 16-bit integer sensors; its forecasting 
predictors degrade on high-entropy 64-bit float streams. 
Hwang et al. introduced bit-depth compression for time-series 
sensor data using zigzag residual mapping with dynamic sub-
bit packing [14]; the technique improves spatial efficiency but 
remains strictly lossless and does not adapt model geometry. 
Sun et al.'s Shrink [10] performs semantic extraction by fitting 
linear segments under dynamic error thresholds; it captures 
broad patterns effectively but cannot encode random float64 
residuals as compactly as XOR-Delta or bitpack-based 
residual coding. 
D. Edge IIoT and Prediction-Based Reduction 
Yang et al. proposed an edge data-management framework 
for IIoT that combines data partitioning with change-point 
detection for real-time compression of unstable industrial 
streams [15]; the framework lacks a strict bitpacking or varint 
pipeline to squash residuals to a minimum byte footprint. Liu 
et al.'s Xender [16] uses sampling-then-prediction to 
accelerate IoT delivery but does not adaptively switch 

=== PAGE 4 ===
between bitwise and polynomial models, leading to poor 
accuracy on volatile float-typed telemetry. 
E. Cross-Domain Benchmarking and Standards 
Chen et al.'s FCBench [17] provides a roofline-model 
evaluation of 13 lossless floating-point compressors across 
CPU and GPU implementations, conclusively demonstrating 
that neural compressors, while compact, are too slow for real-
time edge deployment. The benchmark establishes the 
throughput envelope inside which any practical floating-point 
compressor must operate: between roughly 50 MB/s and 
several GB/s. Underlying all of the above are the foundational 
standards: IEEE 754 [2] for the float layout, RFC 1951 
DEFLATE [18] for byte-level back-end compression, the 
Welford online variance algorithm [19] for numerically stable 
streaming statistics, and the Levenberg–Marquardt method 
[20] for nonlinear least-squares model fitting  all of which 
DLC builds upon. 
F. Where the Gaps Are 
Reading across the five threads above, the gaps DLC tries 
to close are these: 
1) The lossless paradox. Gorilla and Chimp must encode the 
noise floor faithfully because they refuse to lose any bit. 
On real sensor traces this is the dominant constraint on 
their ratio. 
2) Neural overhead. Recent learned compressors are very 
compact but typically operate in the KB/s regime  many 
orders of magnitude under what an ingestion pipeline can 
afford. 
3) Inflexibility of a single predictor. Every prior scheme 
commits to one predictor for the whole stream (XOR, 
linear, sampled, or a fixed polynomial), which is the 
wrong choice on signals whose character changes 
between segments. 
4) Parallelism almost as an afterthought. Most published 
reference implementations either run single-threaded or 
are written in Python and bottle-necked on the GIL. 
5) No dynamic bound on quantization error. Lossy 
compressors expose a single global error setting; window-
local data with very different residual scales pays the 
same precision regardless. Without a derived lower bound 
on the precision field it is also easy to violate the contract 
on outlier windows. 
Each of the five gaps lines up against a specific component 
of the DLC pipeline: the multi-model selector, the trial 
encoder, the per-window precision field, the std::async 
dispatcher, and the derived lower bound p_floor = 16 that ties 
window-level precision to the global 8×10⁻⁶ contract. Section 
III walks through the components in order. 
III. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE AND METHODOLOGY 
Internally, the engine is best understood at two levels. The 
pipeline view in Fig. 1 enumerates the five compression stages

A. Stage 1 Block Chunking and Parallel Dispatch 
Let the input be y = (y₀, y₁, …, y_{N−1}) ∈ ℝᴺ, with each 
yᵢ an IEEE 754 float64. The signal is partitioned into fixed-
size blocks of B = 100,000 samples (configurable via --chunk-
size): 
𝓑_k = (y_{kB}, y_{kB+1}, …, y_{min((k+1)B, N)−1}),  k = 0, 
1, …, ⌈N/B⌉ − 1. 

=== PAGE 5 ===
Blocks are dispatched independently to a worker pool. 
Workers may finish in any order, but their outputs are 
collected in strict block-index order before payload 
concatenation. This guarantees deterministic byte-identical 
output regardless of the number of threads or the order of 
completion. 
The 
Python 
implementation 
uses 
concurrent.futures.ThreadPoolExecutor, while the C++17 
production engine uses std::async(std::launch::async, …) with 
an explicit std::future collection loop. The number of workers 
is capped at min(requested, hardware_concurrency()) to 
prevent oversubscription. 
B. Stage 2 Adaptive Variance-Guided Windowing 
Each block 𝓑_k is segmented into a sequence of variable-
length windows (W₀, W₁, …, W_{m−1}) of statistically 
homogeneous variance. Window sizes lie in [W_min, 
W_max(|𝓑_k|)] where W_min = 16 and the maximum is 
adaptive: 
W_max(B) = 512  if B < 5,000;  1024  if 5,000 ≤ B < 100,000;  
4096  if B ≥ 100,000. 
Variance is computed using Welford's numerically stable 
single-pass algorithm [19]. Let σ²(W) denote the population 
variance of a window. The per-block global variance is σ²_g = 
σ²(𝓑_k). For each candidate starting position i, an initial 
variance σ²_i is computed over the first W_min samples; the 
variance threshold is 
T = max(σ²_i · α_local, σ²_g · α_global), 
with α_local = 10.0 and α_global = 0.10 (tuned 
empirically). The window is then grown by greedy doubling 
each iteration doubles the candidate window length while 
σ²(W) ≤ T bounded above by W_max. A stagnation guard 
aborts expansion when σ²(W_{2k}) / σ²(W_k) > 1.5, i.e. the 
variance jumps too sharply between successive doublings, 
signalling that the signal has changed character (e.g., a 
transient or step). The exact split boundary is then refined 
within the last doubling interval [W_k, W_{2k}] by binary 
search to locate the maximal window size that still satisfies the 
threshold. A fast path emits the entire block as one window 
when the global variance is below ε_const = 10⁻²⁰, capturing 
truly constant regions efficiently. 
C. Stage 3  Multi-Model Selection 
For each emitted window W of length n, six candidate 
predictive models are fitted in parallel and the one minimizing 
the 
Sum-of-Squared-Residuals 
is 
selected, 
subject 
to 
preference rules favouring simpler models. Indexing samples 
by x = 0, 1, …, n−1: 
Linear (model_id = 0). Closed-form least-squares fit ŷ = 
mx + c: 
m = (n Σxy − Σx Σy) / (n Σx² − (Σx)²),    c = (Σy − m Σx) / n. 
Quadratic (model_id = 1). ŷ = ax² + bx + c, solved via 3×3 
normal equations using Cramer's rule (no external linear-
algebra library required). 
XOR-Delta (model_id = 2). A bit-exact lossless predictor: 
anchor = y₀,    rᵢ = bits(yᵢ) ⊕ bits(yᵢ₋₁), i ≥ 1, 
where bits(·) denotes the IEEE 754 float64-to-uint64 
reinterpretation. 
In 
Python 
this 
is 
struct.unpack('<Q', 
struct.pack('<d', v))[0]; in C++ it is std::memcpy(&u64, &dbl, 
8). This model is forced whenever the window contains NaN, 
±Inf, or subnormal values, since it is the only safe fully-
lossless option. 
Constant (model_id = 3). ŷ = c with c = mean(y). Single-
parameter, ideal for step functions and flat regions. 
Sinusoidal (model_id = 4). ŷ = A · sin(ωx + φ) + DC. The 
dominant frequency is seeded by an FFT of the mean-removed 
signal, 
f̂₀ = (1/n) · argmax_{k>0} | ℱ{y − ȳ}(k) |, 
then the four parameters (A, ω, φ, DC) are refined by 
Levenberg–Marquardt iterations on the Jacobian J = 
[sin(ωx+φ), Ax cos(ωx+φ), A cos(ωx+φ), 1], solving the 
damped normal equation (JᵀJ + λI) δ = Jᵀr with λ = 10⁻⁴. The 
C++ port implements its own O(n) DFT and 4×4 Gaussian 
elimination, requiring no external library. If Sinusoidal SSR 
exceeds the linear baseline, the model falls back to linear to 
guarantee monotonic improvement. 
Predictive-XOR (model_id = 5). Stores two anchors y₀, y₁ 
and predicts each subsequent sample by linear extrapolation, 
then XORs the bit pattern: 
ŷᵢ = 2yᵢ₋₁ − yᵢ₋₂,    rᵢ = bits(yᵢ) ⊕ bits(ŷᵢ), i ≥ 2. 
This is a lossless predictor that captures linearly trending 
noisy data better than plain XOR-Delta. 
Selection rule. Let S_M denote the SSR (or popcount-of-
XOR proxy for bit-domain models) for model M. The selector 
first finds the best analytical model M* ∈ {Linear, Quadratic, 
Constant, Sinusoidal} by argmin S_M. A constant preference 
applies: if S_Constant ≤ S_{M*} · 1.01, the Constant model 
wins. Then the better XOR variant is identified between XOR-
Delta and Pred-XOR. Finally, an XOR preference applies: if 
S_XOR_best ≤ S_{M*} · 1.02, the lossless XOR variant is 
preferred (zero quantization error). Otherwise the analytical 
winner is used. 
D. Stage 4 Quantization and Multi-Strategy Entropy 
Encoding 
For analytical models, the residuals r = y − ŷ ∈ ℝⁿ are 
quantized to integer multiples of 2⁻ᵖ: 

=== PAGE 6 ===
qᵢ = round(rᵢ · 2ᵖ),    r̃ᵢ = qᵢ · 2⁻ᵖ, 
with reconstruction error per sample bounded by |rᵢ − r̃ᵢ| ≤ 
2⁻⁽ᵖ⁺¹⁾. The adaptive precision selector chooses p per window: 
p* = min(p_max, max(p_floor, min(p_req, p_adapt))), 
where p_max = 20, p_floor = ⌈log₂(1/8 × 10⁻⁶) − 1⌉ = 16, 
p_req is the user-requested precision, and the adaptive 
component is 
p_adapt = max(p_min, ⌈ −log₂(maxᵢ |rᵢ| + ε) ⌉ + 4), 
with p_min = 8 and ε = 10⁻³⁰. For windows with max|rᵢ| < 
10⁻¹⁵ (essentially zero residuals), p* = p_min. The quantized 
integers qᵢ ∈ ℤ are then trial-encoded by all six strategies, and 
the smallest output is retained. 
Strategy 0 Zigzag-Varint. Signed-to-unsigned mapping z 
= (n ≪ 1) ⊕ (n ≫ 63), followed by base-128 varint with a 7-
bit continuation flag. 
Strategy 1 Fixed-Width Bitpack. All zigzag-encoded 
values are packed at uniform bit-width w = ⌈log₂(maxᵢ zᵢ + 1)⌉, 
LSB-first within each byte. A 1-byte header stores w. 
Strategy 2 Delta-of-Residuals. First-order differences Δᵢ 
= qᵢ − qᵢ₋₁ followed by Zigzag-Varint. Beneficial when 
residuals are themselves smooth. 
Strategy 3 MAD Outlier Separation. The Median 
Absolute Deviation MAD = medianᵢ |qᵢ − median(q)| is 
computed. Values satisfying |qᵢ − median(q)| > 4.5 · MAD are 
classified as outliers and stored as (uint32_le index, 
varint(zigzag(value))) pairs in a separate table; the remaining 
inliers are bitpacked at a reduced width derived from their 
own zigzag maximum. Outperforms uniform bitpack on 
signals with occasional spikes in otherwise smooth data. 
Strategy 4 Run-Length Encoding (RLE). Encodes runs 
of 
identical 
quantized 
values 
as 
(count_of_runs, 
[(zigzag(value), run_length)]) with all numbers as varints. 
Dominates on Constant-model windows where most residuals 
are zero. 
Strategy 
5 
Delta-of-Delta 
(DoD). 
Second-order 
differencing with Zigzag-Varint. Equivalent in spirit to the 
timestamp encoding of InfluxDB and Prometheus, applied 
here to the quantized residual stream: δᵢ = qᵢ − qᵢ₋₁, δδᵢ = δᵢ − 
δᵢ₋₁. 
Block-level DoD fast path. Before the per-window 
pipeline executes, the entire block is evaluated as a single 
Constant(mean) window encoded with DoD. If this 
representation is smaller than the windowed assembly, it is 
used directly, eliminating per-window metadata overhead. 
This makes DLC a functional superset of standalone DoD 
encoders: whenever DoD is the optimal representation, DLC 
selects it, and otherwise DLC may improve upon it via 
windowing. 
For XOR-domain models (Strategies 2 and 5 of Stage 3), 
residuals are already 64-bit unsigned XOR values. They are 
encoded raw or, when beneficial, as cumulative deltas-of-
XOR with a 1-byte prefix flag (0x00 raw, 0x01 delta). 
E. Stage 5 Binary Assembly and Integrity 
The per-window encoded blocks are concatenated into an 
uncompressed payload, each prefixed by a 7-byte window 
header (uint32 window_length, uint8 model_id, uint8 
encoding_id, uint8 precision_bits) followed by N · 8 bytes of 
float64-LE model parameters and a uint32 residual_length 
then the residual byte stream. The complete payload is zlib-
deflated [18] at compression level 6. The on-disk file format is 
shown in Fig. 3. 
 
Fig. 3. .dlc binary file layout. A 64-byte header carries magic bytes, version, 
and global metadata; the zlib-compressed payload contains the per-window 
blocks; the 8-byte footer stores a CRC-32 over the uncompressed payload for 
integrity validation. 
Integrity is verified on read by the magic bytes (header and 
footer), the uncompressed-size field, and a CRC-32 [21] over 
the uncompressed payload. The Python implementation 
explicitly masks the CRC with & 0xFFFFFFFF to guarantee 
unsigned 32-bit semantics matching the C++ crc32() from 
zlib.h. 
F. Modelling Assumptions (Mathematical) 
The pipeline above leans on a small set of mathematical 
and signal-level assumptions. They are not buried in code 
paths; they are the reason the design works at all, and the 
reason we can put a hard number on the error bound. We list 

=== PAGE 7 ===
them here so a reader can see, in one place, what we are taking 
for granted. 
A1 IEEE 754 binary64, little-endian on disk. Every numeric 
value entering and leaving the engine is a 64-bit IEEE 754 
double. Reinterpretation between double and uint64 is bit-
exact 
(memcpy 
in 
C++, 
struct.unpack('<Q', 
struct.pack('<d', v))[0] in Python). The on-disk byte order 
for both models and residuals is little-endian; cross-
platform decode relies on this being a fixed convention 
rather than a host attribute. 
A2 Mantissa noise dominates the bottom bits. The lower 
~30–40 mantissa bits of a continuous physical signal are 
dominated by ADC quantization residue and hardware 
thermal noise, and behave as approximately statistically 
independent samples from one tick to the next. This is 
what makes them irrecoverable for a dictionary coder and 
what justifies treating them as loss-bearing in the 
precision controller. 
A3 Slow variation in the high-order mantissa. By contrast, 
the upper mantissa bits of successive samples are highly 
correlated for the signals we target (temperature, pressure, 
current, vibration envelope, smooth ramps). Closed-form 
predictors with a constant, linear, or quadratic shape are 
therefore expected to capture most of the per-window 
variance which is what the SSR selector empirically 
confirms on real data. 
A4 Block independence under the chosen split. Blocks of B 
= 100,000 samples are treated as independent units of 
work. There is no model state, no predictor history, and 
no entropy table that crosses a block boundary. This is 
what allows deterministic parallel dispatch and is also 
why thread count cannot perturb the compressed bytes. 
A5 Finite, well-defined window variance. Welford's 
algorithm is invoked on each candidate window assuming 
finite first and second moments. Windows containing 
NaN, ±Inf, or subnormal values violate this assumption; 
the engine detects them and forces the lossless XOR-
Delta predictor (model_id 2) for those windows, 
sidestepping the variance pipeline entirely. 
A6 SSR is a usable proxy for compressed size. Stage 3 picks 
a model by minimum Sum-of-Squared-Residuals, not by 
trial-encoding all six and measuring bytes out. The 
assumption is that lower SSR yields a tighter zigzag-
varint distribution and therefore a smaller residual stream. 
We tested this empirically on the 21-dataset suite; the 
SSR-best model agrees with the size-best model in every 
case we measured. 
A7 Strict precision floor enforces the global bound. The 
8×10⁻⁶ per-sample error contract is enforced by clamping 
the per-window precision below at p_floor = 16 bits. The 
bound holds because the worst-case quantization error of 
an n-bit fixed-point coder over the observed dynamic 
range is bounded analytically; a precision below 16 bits 
would not satisfy that bound for any of the 21 datasets, 
which is why p_floor cannot be lowered without re-
deriving the contract. 
Two of these (A2, A3) are empirical claims about the 
signal class the engine targets; the rest are properties of the 
format or the algorithm itself. We flag this distinction because 
A2 and A3 are also the place where the engine will lose: a 
stream where mantissa noise correlates across samples, or 
where the high-order bits do not vary smoothly, breaks the 
rationale for SSR-based predictor selection. The two datasets 
on which DLC loses real_ec2_network_4k (to ZLIB) and 
real_nyc_taxi_10k (to Gorilla) are exactly such streams. 
IV. IMPLEMENTATION 
A. Dual-Language Architecture 
DLC is delivered as two complete implementations sharing 
one binary specification. 
Python prototype (python/dlc/) is a pure-Python reference 
using NumPy for vectorized arithmetic. Its purpose is rapid 
algorithm iteration, property-based fuzz testing, and serving as 
the canonical specification of the .dlc format. Threading uses 
concurrent.futures.ThreadPoolExecutor, 
which 
dispatches 
blocks to interpreter threads but is bounded by the Global 
Interpreter Lock for CPU-bound work; throughput plateaus 
near 4–21 MB/s regardless of thread count. 
C++17 production engine (cpp/) is a header-and-source 
library compiled with CMake against zlib, with no other 
external 
dependencies. 
Parallelism 
uses 
std::async(std::launch::async, …) with explicit std::future 
collection; this bypasses the GIL and scales near-linearly on 
large blocks. The C++ port implements its own O(n) discrete 
Fourier transform and 4×4 Gaussian elimination for the 
Sinusoidal model, eliminating any LAPACK/Eigen/FFTW 
dependency. 
B. Cross-Language Compatibility Contract 
Three byte-level invariants enforced throughout the 
codebase preserve .dlc interoperability: 
1) Unsigned CRC masking. Python zlib.crc32(payload) & 
0xFFFFFFFF matches C++ crc32(0L, …) from <zlib.h>. 
Without 
the 
mask, 
Python 
2/3 
differences 
in 
signed/unsigned return semantics would silently produce 
mismatched footers. 
2) 
Float64 
↔ 
uint64 
reinterpretation. 
Python 
struct.unpack('<Q', struct.pack('<d', v))[0] is bit-exact 

=== PAGE 8 ===
with C++ std::memcpy(&u, &v, 8). Both rely on the 
platform's IEEE 754 float64 being little-endian, which all 
DLC target platforms (x86-64, ARM64) provide. 
3) Pairwise summation in mean computation. The C++ block-
mean used in the DoD fast path is computed by recursive 
pairwise 
summation 
matching 
NumPy's 
np.mean 
accuracy. Naive forward accumulation would diverge by 
up to one ULP on smooth ramps, breaking byte-identity 
of the resulting Constant-model parameter. 
A 6-test cross-language suite verifies in continuous 
integration that (a) Python compress → C++ decompress 
round-trips within bound, (b) C++ compress → Python 
decompress round-trips within bound, and (c) .dlc files are 
byte-identical for closed-form-model signals (Constant, 
Linear, Step). For signals dominated by the iterative 
Sinusoidal model, the two implementations may converge to 
parameters differing by one ULP, in which case files are not 
byte-identical but cross-decode is always within the 8×10⁻⁶ 
bound. 
C. Determinism and Thread Safety 
All shared state in both engines is read-only after 
construction; no locks are required. Result ordering is 
enforced 
by 
collecting 
std::futures 
(or 
concurrent.futures.Futures) in the order they were submitted, 
not the order they complete. The Python and C++ codepaths 
therefore both produce deterministic, thread-count-invariant 
output.

A. Adaptive Geometry via Six Models with SSR Selection 
Prior work uses a single prediction strategy: Gorilla and 
Chimp use XOR universally; Sprintz uses delta-coded 
forecasting; SZ3 uses a fixed local predictor per pipeline 
stage. DLC carries six models simultaneously and selects the 
local optimum per window by SSR, with explicit preference 
for simpler (fewer-parameter) and lossless (XOR-family) 
models when they are nearly tied. Empirically this lets DLC 
achieve 
101.9× 
compression 
on 
smooth 
sines 
(Linear/Sinusoidal-dominated), 2355.7× on linear ramps 
(Constant/Linear-dominated), and 3.0× on cumulative random 
walks (Pred-XOR/XOR-Delta-dominated) within a single 
algorithm. 
B. Per-Window Adaptive Precision 

=== PAGE 9 ===
To our knowledge, no prior float-time-series compressor 
stores a precision field per window. Storing one uint8 per 
window (8–20 bits) lets smooth windows quantize to 8 bits 
while spiky windows hold 16-bit precision under the same 
global error contract. The lower bound p_floor = 16 is derived 
from the documented error bound |r| < 8×10⁻⁶, not chosen 
heuristically. 
C. Six-Strategy Trial Encoding with DoD Superset Property 
Trial-encoding by all strategies and picking the smallest is 
feasible because the encoders are all O(n) and most windows 
are ≤ 4096 samples. Including Delta-of-Delta as a native 
strategy ensures DLC strictly dominates standalone DoD 
encoders on every window where DoD is the local optimum. 
The block-level DoD fast path further ensures DLC dominates 
standalone DoD even at the whole-block granularity, 
eliminating the per-window metadata overhead in cases where 
the block has no useful sub-structure. 
D. Strict Cross-Language Byte-Level Specification 
Most published compressors are released as a single 
reference implementation; cross-language compatibility is 
rarely a stated guarantee. DLC's specification is enforced by a 
6-test cross-language CI suite covering Python → C++, C++ 
→ Python, and byte-identity for closed-form-model signals. 
The .dlc format's 64-byte header, 8-byte CRC-32 footer, and 
per-window 
precision_bits 
field 
are 
designed 
to 
be 
unambiguously serialized in both languages. 
E. True Parallelism Without Synchronization Cost 
Because all shared state is immutable post-segmentation, 
neither engine requires locks or atomics. Determinism is 
recovered solely through the order in which futures are 
collected. This makes DLC's parallel speedup essentially free 
of synchronization overhead. 
VI. EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION 
A. Datasets 
We evaluate on 21 binary float64 datasets covering 
synthetic edge cases, real-world telemetry, large-scale 
industrial simulations, and round-trip recovery signals. 
Synthetic datasets include 1-million-sample sine, linear ramp, 
cumulative random walk, multi-frequency noisy sine, 
heartbeat, vibration, industrial ramp, Lorenz attractor (500 K 
samples), and a 3-million-sample industrial sensor simulation. 
Real-world datasets include the AWS EC2 CPU utilization (4 
032 samples), AWS EC2 network (4 730 samples), machine 
temperature (22 695 samples), NYC taxi demand (10 320 
samples), Twitter volume (15 902 samples), and ambient 
temperature (7 267 samples) from the Numenta Anomaly 
Benchmark [11], and the Jena Climate temperature and 
pressure records (420 551 samples each) from atmospheric 
station data. Two round-trip recovery datasets test_recovered 
(1 000 000 samples) and test_rt_rec (3 000 000 samples) 
exercise the decompress-then-recompress pipeline and verify 
that DLC output is stable under repeated encoding.

at 16-bit precision, Run-Length Encoding (RLE), and GZIP 
and ZLIB at compression level 6 over the raw byte stream of 
the float64 array. For each dataset we measure: (i)

Table IV reports the maximum per-sample reconstruction 
error for every method on all 21 datasets. All 21 datasets 
satisfy the <8×10⁻⁶ bound. Cross-language round-trip testing 
(Python compress → C++ decompress and vice versa) 
confirms that both engines remain within the bound 
symmetrically. The Gorilla, RLE, GZIP and ZLIB baselines 
are lossless (0 exact error) by definition; DoD at 16-bit 
precision matches DLC's error regime (~7.6×10⁻⁶); DoD at 12-
bit precision incurs substantially higher error (~1.2×10⁻⁴),

confirming that the 16-bit floor is essential for the strict 
bound.

VII. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 
A. Where the Strict Error Bound Applies 
The 8×10⁻⁶ per-sample guarantee depends on a clean 
numerical setup: the quantization step at the floor precision 
must not exceed what float64 can represent exactly. Float64 
has integer-exact range up to 2⁵³, around 9×10¹⁵, so 
multiplying a residual by 2¹⁶ has to land inside that. Working 
backwards, that means |rᵢ| < 2³⁷, which is roughly 1.4×10¹¹. 
Any sensor recording we have ever tested sits well below this 
temperatures, currents, packet rates, demand counts. The fuzz 
suite formalises the boundary: with |v| ≤ 10⁶ the bound must 
hold; outside that, or in the presence of NaN/Inf, the engine is 
required to round-trip without crashing but the error contract is 
not asserted. 
B. Why Sinusoidal Windows Are Not Byte-Identical 
Closed-form windows (Linear, Quadratic, Constant) 
produce byte-identical output between Python and C++ 
because the underlying fits are deterministic and the pairwise 
summation we use for the mean matches np.mean to the ULP. 
The Sinusoidal model is different: it runs a Levenberg–
Marquardt loop seeded by an FFT peak, and the 
transcendental primitives plus the 4×4 Gaussian elimination 
drift by single-ULP amounts between numpy and our hand-
rolled C++ kernels. The four parameters can therefore land at 
slightly different local minima. The weaker contract cross-
decode within the 8×10⁻⁶ bound still holds, but byte-identity 
does not. Closing this gap would require either pinning both 
languages to a deterministic numeric library or writing a 
portable bit-exact DFT and matrix solver from scratch. 
C. Where DLC Loses 
The two defeats in our table are worth dwelling on. The 
first, real_ec2_network_4k, is a vector of network-packet 
counters re-cast as float64. Most of the byte-level structure 
comes from the integer-cast itself, and ZLIB's dictionary 
catches it (8.5× vs. DLC's 5.7×). Our mathematical-domain 
predictors do not: there is no smooth underlying function to 
fit. The second defeat, real_nyc_taxi_10k, is a low-magnitude 
taxi-demand signal (10 320 integer-like ride counts stored as 
float64). Gorilla's XOR encoding exploits the fact that 
successive integer-valued samples share most of their mantissa 
bits, achieving 3.6× vs. DLC's 3.4×. In both cases the signal is 
not a continuous physical process it is a discretised counter 
and the Entropy Trap argument does not strictly apply. The 
pragmatic response is to fingerprint the first block of an 
unknown stream, see whether the raw float64 bytes look 
dictionary-compressible or integer-valued, and fall back to a 
specialised encoder for that minority of streams. 
D. Throughput 
Throughput is dominated by the C++ engine and scales 
near-linearly with cores on large blocks; on 8 MB datasets we 
observe ~13 MB/s single-threaded and proportionally higher 
with multiple workers. The Python implementation, GIL-
bound, plateaus around 4–21 MB/s. DLC's design avoids the 
KB/s throughput regime of neural compressors [17] but does 
not yet match the GB/s of vectorized integer codecs [22]; 
SIMD acceleration of the bitpack and varint inner loops is an 
obvious future improvement. 
VIII. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 
DLC is, in the end, a fairly simple idea pushed through 
several layers of engineering. Stop trying to compress raw 
float64 bytes; predict the signal, quantize the residual to 
whatever precision the local data permits, run a small handful 
of entropy coders against the result, and write whichever one 
comes out shortest. Add a block-level DoD shortcut for the 
cases where windowing only adds overhead, wrap the whole 
thing in a binary format with a CRC footer, and write the same 
algorithm twice once in Python for clarity and once in C++17 
for throughput. On 21 datasets that span linear ramps, smooth 
sines, Lorenz attractors, real EC2 counters, atmospheric 
records from Jena, and round-trip recovery signals, the engine 
writes a smaller file than every baseline Gorilla, DoD (16-bit), 
RLE, GZIP and ZLIB on nineteen of them, all while keeping 
per-sample reconstruction error inside 8×10⁻⁶. The two losses 
are an integer-derived network trace (ZLIB wins) and a low-
magnitude 
taxi-demand 
signal 
(Gorilla 
wins), 
both 
representing 
non-physical 
counter 
data 
where 
DLC's 
mathematical predictors offer no advantage. 
We see four threads worth pulling on next. The first is 
throughput: the bitpack and varint inner loops are obvious 
SIMD candidates and would lift the C++ engine from low tens 
of MB/s into the GB/s range that vectorised integer codecs 
already live in [22]. The second is making the Sinusoidal 
model bit-deterministic across languages, which would restore 
byte-identity for every model rather than only the closed-form 
ones. The third is a streaming, append-only .dlc variant for 

=== PAGE 12 ===
unbounded ingestion (the current format assumes a finite 
input). The fourth is auto-detection: if the first block of an 
input stream looks dictionary-friendly or integer-valued rather 
than mathematically-structured, hand the rest of the stream to 
a generic byte coder or Gorilla's XOR encoder and skip the 
DLC pipeline entirely. The Entropy Trap is real, but a sensible 
engine should know when it does not apply  and the two losses 
in this evaluation point directly to the signals that auto-
detection should catch.
